# **Advanced Mobile and Wearable Paradigms for Alaryngeal Communication: The Smartphone as an Electrolarynx and Beyond**

## **Introduction to Alaryngeal Rehabilitation and the Acoustic Paradigm**

The human voice is a highly complex acoustic signal generated by the aerodynamic excitation of the vocal folds within the larynx, which is subsequently modulated by the resonating cavities of the vocal tract, including the pharynx, oral cavity, and nasal passages. For individuals diagnosed with advanced laryngeal carcinoma, or those suffering from severe laryngeal trauma, a total laryngectomy is often the definitive, life-saving surgical intervention. This highly invasive procedure entails the complete anatomical removal of the larynx, effectively separating the upper airway from the digestive tract and permanently redirecting the trachea to a stoma in the anterior neck to facilitate respiration. While this surgical necessity preserves the life of the patient, it simultaneously strips them of their biological sound source, resulting in profound aphonia.

Historically, the electrolarynx (EL) has remained a foundational, universally accessible tool in vocal rehabilitation. The electrolarynx is a handheld, battery-operated external medical device equipped with a rigid vibrating diaphragm. When the device is activated and pressed firmly against the submandibular or cervical tissue (the cheek or neck), it introduces a mechanical acoustic vibration into the hypopharynx and oral cavity. The user then articulates normally, utilizing their lips, teeth, tongue, and palate to shape these raw mechanical vibrations into intelligible speech formants.

Despite iterative engineering improvements, the traditional electrolarynx continues to suffer from severe, inherent acoustic limitations. The resulting speech is notoriously monotonic, mechanical, and perpetually "robotic." As mobile computing power has grown exponentially over the past decade, the paradigm of alaryngeal communication has begun a rapid, transformative shift. The modern Android smartphone presents an unprecedented opportunity to redefine the electrolarynx through direct haptic actuation, the highly specific low-latency repurposing of consumer bone conduction headphones, and real-time artificial intelligence (AI) voice interpretation at the edge.

## **Direct Smartphone Actuation: Emitting Vibration Against the Cheek or Neck**

The most literal interpretation of "using a smartphone as an electrolarynx" involves co-opting the device's internal vibration motor—typically utilized for haptic feedback and silent notifications—to function as an external mechanical sound source. By running a specialized Android application, a laryngectomee can press the flat chassis of the smartphone directly against their neck or cheek, utilizing the phone's internal electromechanical actuators to inject acoustic energy into their vocal tract.

### **Hardware Limitations and Actuator Types**

Modern Android smartphones typically employ one of two types of internal vibration motors, which drastically affects software implementation:

* **Eccentric Rotating Mass (ERM) Motors:** These use an off-center mass that rotates to create a rumble. By varying the applied voltage, the speed of the DC motor changes, which directly alters the vibration frequency (e.g., a motor spinning at 6,000 RPM produces a 100 Hz vibration). ERMs typically operate across a broad frequency range of 30 Hz to 500 Hz.  
* **Linear Resonant Actuators (LRAs):** Found in most modern premium Android smartphones, LRAs consist of a magnetic mass suspended by springs, driven by a voice coil. While they offer rapid start and stop times (10 to 20 milliseconds), they are highly resonant. Typical LRAs are physically optimized to operate at peak output acceleration only within very narrow frequency bands, usually around 150 Hz, or between 200 Hz and 300 Hz.

Human glottal pulses typically range from 100 Hz to over 300 Hz. Driving an LRA away from its precise physical resonant frequency dramatically reduces its mechanical vibration amplitude, making custom frequency generation a complex software challenge.

### **Explicit Android API Implementation for Frequency Modulation**

To build an Android application that turns the smartphone into a vibrating sound source, developers must bypass standard notification patterns and seize continuous control of the haptic hardware. First, the app must declare \<uses-permission android:name="android.permission.VIBRATE"/\> in the AndroidManifest.xml.

Historically, developers used VibrationEffect.createWaveform(long timings, int amplitudes, int repeat) to create a pseudo-frequency by rapidly alternating the motor on and off. However, Android has introduced advanced haptic primitives specifically for fine-grained frequency control:

* **WaveformEnvelopeBuilder (Android 11+ & Android 16):** Starting with Android 11 (and heavily refined in Android 16 / API 36 with the introduction of VibratorFrequencyProfile), developers can explicitly command both the amplitude and the target frequency in Hertz over a specific duration.  
* **Code Implementation:** A continuous, pitch-adjustable hum can be programmed utilizing the VibrationEffect.WaveformEnvelopeBuilder. To synthesize a continuous 120 Hz buzz, the developer constructs the effect by adding control points:  
  Java  
  VibrationEffect effect \= new VibrationEffect.WaveformEnvelopeBuilder()  
     .addControlPoint(1.0f, 120f, 100) // Ramp to full amplitude at 120 Hz over 100ms  
     .addControlPoint(1.0f, 120f, 200) // Hold full amplitude at 120 Hz for 200ms  
     .addControlPoint(0.0f, 120f, 100) // Ramp down to 0 over 100ms  
     .build();  
  vibrator.vibrate(effect);

  This addControlPoint(float amplitude, float frequency, long duration) method allows the app to dynamically sweep the frequency up and down based on a user's finger sliding across the touchscreen, mimicking the natural prosody (pitch variation) of human speech.

## **Repurposing Bone Conduction Headphones for Speech Generation**

Given the acoustic impedance mismatch of a flat smartphone chassis, a vastly superior approach is the software-driven repurposing of consumer bone conduction headphones (such as Shokz).

Bone conduction technology bypasses the eardrum entirely; the devices utilize specialized piezoelectric or electromagnetic transducers to convert electrical signals into intense mechanical oscillations. When an app drives these headsets with raw vocal waveforms, and the user presses the transducers against the soft tissue of the anterior neck, the mechanical energy is successfully injected directly into the vocal tract.

### **Advanced Acoustic Waveform Synthesis (The Rosenberg Glottal Pulse)**

To function as an electrolarynx, the app cannot simply play a sine wave; a sine wave contains only a fundamental frequency and no harmonics, rendering articulated speech completely unintelligible. The software must mathematically synthesize a "glottal pulse"—a waveform that models the sudden closing and gradual opening of human vocal folds.

A standard implementation utilizes the **Rosenberg Type C Glottal Pulse**. ### **Low-Latency C++ Oboe Implementation**

Generating this Rosenberg pulse and sending it to a Bluetooth headset via the standard Java AudioTrack API results in massive buffering latency, severely disrupting conversational timing. Android developers must write the audio synthesis engine in C/C++ using the open-source **Oboe** library (which leverages the ultra-low-latency AAudio C API introduced in Android 8.0).

* **Implementing the Callback:** The core of the application must extend the oboe::AudioStreamDataCallback class. The developer overrides the onAudioReady method to populate the hardware audio buffer with the mathematically synthesized Rosenberg pulse in real-time:  
  C++  
  class GlottalPulseSynthesizer : public oboe::AudioStreamDataCallback {  
      oboe::DataCallbackResult onAudioReady(oboe::AudioStream \*oboeStream, void \*audioData, int32\_t numFrames) override {

              floatData\[i\] \= currentPulseValue;

          return oboe::DataCallbackResult::Continue;  
      }  
  };

* **Audio Routing via AudioManager (API 31+):** Once the C++ engine is generating the pulse, the Java layer must force the audio out to the paired bone conduction headset, bypassing default system speakers. In Android 12 (API 31+), the legacy startBluetoothSco() method is heavily restricted. Instead, developers must use AudioManager.setCommunicationDevice(). The app must query audioManager.getAvailableCommunicationDevices(), identify the connected headset by checking for AudioDeviceInfo.TYPE\_BLUETOOTH\_A2DP or TYPE\_BLE\_HEADSET, and route the stream directly to it.

### **Hardware Impedance Limitations for DIY Transducers**

While commercial wireless bone conduction headphones contain their own matched internal amplifiers, developers attempting to wire raw, bare bone conduction transducers (e.g., Adafruit 1W transducers) directly into an Android smartphone's 3.5mm or USB-C audio DAC face severe electrical limitations.

Raw bone conduction transducers typically possess an extremely low impedance of 8 ohms. Smartphone audio outputs are engineered to drive standard earbuds with an impedance of 32 ohms. Attempting to drive an 8-ohm transducer directly from a smartphone forces the phone's internal amplifier to attempt to output significantly more current, triggering the smartphone's clipping/current-limit protections and resulting in virtually no vibration. Therefore, wired DIY setups explicitly require routing the smartphone's audio output through an external Class-D micro-amplifier (like a TS2012 or MAX98306 breakout board) to boost the signal before it reaches the transducer.

## **The Smartphone as a Real-Time Interpreter**

The most clinically advanced paradigm involves using the smartphone not as a sound generator, but as a real-time acoustic interpreter. In this configuration, the user holds a traditional hardware electrolarynx against their neck to speak. They simultaneously place their Android smartphone on a table or wear a lapel mic connected to it. The app captures the harsh, robotic speech, cleans it, and translates it into natural text or a cloned human voice in real time.

### **NDK Real-Time Spectral Subtraction (Noise Suppression)**

Electrolarynx speech is saturated with a continuous, low-frequency mechanical buzz radiating directly from the device housing. Because this buzz is continuous and highly localized, standard cloud APIs struggle to transcribe it. Developers must clean the audio locally on the Android device before transcription.

This is achieved by implementing a Real-Time Spectral Subtraction algorithm in C++ via the NDK. The Android device captures microphone input and slices it into **20-millisecond frames** with a **50% overlap**, utilizing Hann analysis/synthesis windows for perfect reconstruction. The algorithm calculates an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) to actively estimate the magnitude spectrum of the mechanical background noise. It then applies a Wiener-style gain to subtract this specific noise signature from the audio frame in real time, drastically improving the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) before passing it to the speech-to-text engine.

### **Low-Latency Cloud STT WebSocket Streaming**

For transcribing the cleaned audio into text captions, standard Android speech recognizers introduce too much latency. Developers integrate enterprise-grade cloud Speech-to-Text (STT) APIs optimized for ultra-low-latency streaming.

* **Deepgram Nova-3:** Currently leading the benchmark for atypical speech and speed, the Deepgram API utilizes a persistent bidirectional WebSocket connection to stream the audio chunks. It achieves a Word Error Rate (WER) of just 5.26% and a native streaming latency of roughly 300 milliseconds.  
* **OpenAI Whisper:** While highly accurate, utilizing standard Whisper architectures via API typically introduces latency measured in seconds, making it unsuitable for live conversation, and it yields a higher baseline WER of 10.6% compared to dedicated real-time models.

### **Real-Time Speech-to-Speech (STS) AI Voice Conversion**

The ultimate evolution of the Android interpreter app is full Speech-to-Speech (STS) voice conversion. Rather than merely displaying text on a screen, the application captures the user's robotic electrolarynx phonemes and synthesizes a natural, personalized human voice in real time.

* **Whispp:** Recognized globally for its accessibility breakthroughs, the Whispp app utilizes an entirely localized, audio-to-audio AI model. By running the neural network directly on the Android device's hardware accelerators (Edge AI), it bypasses the heavy latency of standard Speech-to-Text-to-Speech pipelines, achieving total voice conversion latency of under 100 milliseconds. This ultra-low latency is what allows a laryngectomee to hold fluid, natural phone calls and face-to-face conversations using their original, pre-surgery voice identity.

## **Conclusion**

The Android platform provides an immensely capable, customizable ecosystem for developing next-generation alaryngeal communication tools. By leveraging advanced APIs like WaveformEnvelopeBuilder for precise continuous haptic pulsing, or by utilizing C++ Oboe data callbacks alongside AudioManager.setCommunicationDevice() to route mathematically synthesized Rosenberg glottal waveforms directly to Bluetooth bone conduction headphones, developers can create highly effective software-driven speech prostheses. Furthermore, by integrating NDK-level spectral subtraction and ultra-low-latency Speech-to-Speech AI (such as Whispp), the Android device is empowered to act as a flawless, real-time interpreter, instantly translating the harsh mechanical buzz of a traditional electrolarynx into a natural, expressive human voice.
